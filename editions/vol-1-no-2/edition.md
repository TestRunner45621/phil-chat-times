##### TB Y O U R S O C I A L A F F A I R S D E S K

here is no polite way for a man in this channel to say that he is fond of the other men in it, and shortly after midday on Thursday one of them tried anyway.

Donʼs message was sixtyfour words long, carried a typo he did not correct, and said, in substance, that these people are his only friends, that he has not appreciated them enough, and that he hopes it lasts. He put it into the middle of an argument about whether philosophers are lonelier than other men, which is either the worst or the best available moment, and it sat there for approximately sixty seconds.

Then Charlie posted it back at him. Not a reply to it — it. The whole thing, verbatim, typo included.

Jeh did the same within the minute. Stackhouse two minutes after that. Then the bit appeared to die; ten full minutes passed, which in this channel is a season. Then Hugh revived it, and Sizzurp closed it, and six identical paragraphs of undefended affection stood in the transcript in the space of a quarter of an hour.

This newspaper wishes to record that it was the warmest thing that happened in Phil Chat all week.

We are aware of how it looks. A copypasta is ordinarily a humiliation — the internetʼs method of pinning a manʼs sincerity to a board. That is not what this was, and the evidence is in what failed to happen. Five men were handed a sentiment they had no vocabulary to return, and they returned it in the only currency available, which was the sentiment itself.

That is not mockery. That is antiphony. It is what a congregation does.

We note also, because nobody in the channel did, that Jeh and Stackhouse each spoke exactly once in eight hundred and sixty-six lines of Thursday, and that the once was this.

The Two Defections Consensus here has a half-life of about eleven minutes, and the form began to mutate on schedule.

My Name declined the sentiment and filed its exact negative — the same paragraph inverted into a declaration of enmity, hoping the rivalry continues “throughout the minutes,” and swapping shooting the shit for shooting the president. It is a good joke and it is also, structurally, a seventh confirmation: he could not leave the shape alone

And a Word for the Defence A colleague objects that we have been too generous, and that six men pasting a sincere message back at its author is bullying by any ordinary standard. The objection is fair and we reject it on the evidence.

Bullying has a target and a direction. This had neither. What it had was six men who each, independently, within a minute or two of one another, decided that the correct response to being told they were loved was to say it back — and who then discovered that none of them could construct the sentence himself. They had one available, already written, sitting in the channel. They used it.

The Times will concede this much: the mechanism is deeply broken, and nobody involved would be able to say what he meant if you stopped him and asked. But the men who could not say it at least declined to say anything else. Simonʼs version and My Nameʼs inversion arrived after — after the form had held five times, after the point was made. They were riffs on an established consensus, which in this room is the closest thing available to a signed card.

We note, finally, that not one of the six has mentioned it since, that Don has not raised it again, and that it will never be discussed. That is also how a congregation works.

The Rehearsal,

Two Days Earlier There is a detail that reframes the whole episode and it has gone entirely unremarked, including by us until this week.

On Tuesday just before one oʼclock, Bedbug filed into the channel, without preamble and in the first person, a fourhundred-word account of cocaine, impotence, an AIDS test, a wife who was “tripping,” and a God he suspected of having nothing to do with it. It is a genuinely good piece of writing — the closing movement, in which the author admits he prefers the false explanation to the true one, is better than anything else filed in Phil Chat this week.

Pearl read it and asked what he was yapping about. He replied: “opening up to people.” She asked, quietly, “Youʼre married?” Nobody answered her, then or since.

The film is called Obsession. It is, by the eventual consensus of the people who spent six days fighting about it, fine. This newspaperʼs position, arrived at after reviewing every message, is that the film was never the subject and that everyone involved knew it.

What Phil Chat conducted between the small hours of Saturday and the small hours of Wednesday was a gender argument wearing a horror film as a hat. The question on the docket — whether a man who sleeps with a woman behaving strangely has done something monstrous — is not a question about cinema, and the reason it ran a hundred and thirty hours is that it is not answerable by watching anything. The film simply supplied a defendant who could not sue.

It opened with Sizenberg, conducting his second viewing live and in capitals at the pace of a man being electrocuted at intervals, demanding to know at what point exactly the protagonist becomes a monster. Zone answered him literally — after the dinner scene — and the Times regards this as the single most helpful contribution of the week, because it was the only one that treated the question as having an answer.

aura, watching from the neutral corner, filed the review this newspaper considers definitive: that tearing your shirt off is an S-tier entrance to a schizo rant. Sizenbergʼs clarification, that it had been a real shirt with structure to it, is the sort of detail no editor would dare invent.

Formalised, Which

Solves Nothing The channelʼs instinct, when handed a man screaming about a movie, is to strip out the screaming and hand it back as a numbered proof. synth.wav did precisely this, filing nine premises running from the terms of the protagonistʼs wish through personal identity to consent and back out again by modus tollens.

It is a competent argument. It is also completely inert, and its fate demonstrates why. Zone attacked the fourth premise, then offered a rewrite of the whole thing in five premises of his own, then returned to attack a premise he had already conceded. synth.wavʼs eventual diagnosis — “i fear you accept all the premises” — is the most accurate sentence said about this channel all week and applies to roughly a third of its arguments.

Formalisation here is not a method. It is a costume. It signals that a man is serious without exposing him to the risk of being wrong about anything, and the moment the argument became rigorous it stopped being able to move.

Stackhouse, who understood this, skipped it entirely and laid a trap in five words. He declared the protagonist a rapist, waited for Zone to agree, asked whether he actually believed that — and then, before Zone could answer, supplied “Idiot.” It is not a rebuttal. It worked better than nine premises.

### A Man Brings a Machine to a Fight About Machines

##### TB Y O U R S C I E N C E D E S K

he most technically serious engagement of the week, and the one this newspaper found most uncomfortable to read, ran an hour and forty minutes on Sunday afternoon between vannak and Hugh on the question of whether machine interpretability research interprets anything.

vannak opened by declaring that there are no good papers on mechanistic interpretability, that it is neither mechanistic nor interpretable, and that most of what has been done to these systems is stupid. Hughʼs reply — “not with that attitude” — was the last light thing either of them said.

vannakʼs case is straightforward and, as far as the Times can assess, not obviously wrong. If you replace a nonlinear component with a linear stand-in in order to read it, you have stopped studying the thing you trained. He said it several ways over the afternoon and never once dressed it up: you trained one model and interpreted another; the linear approximation is the second model; you are analysing a system that was never built and never measured.
