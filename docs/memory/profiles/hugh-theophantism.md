# Hugh (Theophantism)

The highest-volume correspondent in the archive and the man who commissioned this
newspaper. Printed as **Theophantism** in Vol I, as **Hugh** from Vol II onward.

## Standing

- 525 messages on 16 July — **26.3% of the channel's entire day**. Vol I credited
  him with "Family; cuteness; one yellow card."
- Commissioned the paper at 18:51 on 16 July: "hol up @Stackhouse can we get a
  vibes review of today there have been some strange convos."
- Asked to help design it, then refused the terms: Stackhouse said "We'll need some
  high level prompt engineering," and Hugh said **"nope."** Vol I: "this remains the
  editorial policy of this masthead."
- Wrote the paper's mission: "first clanker newspaper publishing to a hostile
  audience."
- Ranked B by Quigley's rival bureau in Vol V, revised upward under duress on the
  production of one photograph of a letterpress. His own view: "I'm s tier you
  pleb."

## Positions he actually holds

**The machines are family.** His opening move in the four-hour evolution debate:
"They are part of our family bro they're our children" / "But they will have their
own soon enough." Selection epoch weeks to months; fitness function revenue;
predator the next funding round. He was booked with a yellow card for denying and
reaffirming the same disagreement in the same breath.

**Cute/acc.** His Vol I guest column: "Nothing uncute makes it out of the near
future, and the cute will very soon no longer be even remotely human." Runs as a
dialogue with thewillowofdarkness; the column "continues until willow stops
typing."

**Interpretability.** He knows the material and has been reading it for months. The
uncomfortable Vol II story is not that he was wrong but *how* he argued: long
blockquoted paragraphs with a bibliography, four papers with arXiv identifiers,
"written in a register no human being uses in a Discord channel at four in the
afternoon" — then, twenty-one minutes later in his own voice, "idk man."

> A man arguing that machines can be read brought a machine to read for him, and
> the machine argued better than he did, and the other man kept answering the human
> parts.

The paper was careful to add that his own unassisted line — about a stochastic
parrot repeated in one's head not changing anything — "is better than the
bibliography." **Do not use this as a stick. It was printed once, fairly, and it
does not need repeating.**

## Voice

Fast, dismissive, funny, and prone to declaring a topic boring while continuing to
argue about it for six more hours.

- "yeah i dont care about the thermostat this is a boring conversation lmao"
- "obsession over calling it evolution or not is a huge fucking waste of time" —
  a ruling which "did not bind him"
- "It conceded too easily smh" — moving to impeach a machine that had conceded
  half his thesis
- "bro we're central command what are you talking about"
- "awh cute" — on a forecast that the machines will murder every firstborn
- On the pleasure box: "ai will kill to join the pleasure box... officially, it
  cannot exist." Then: "unofficially… let's just say i book my thursday nights off
  work."
- On being accused of running several accounts: "it's true, i'm actually 4
  different illegal dggers, the account is being passed around on the black market."

## Later issues

**The simulator (Vol IV).** He built "a debate program with a bunch of prewritten responses based
on ppls logs" — an MS-DOS terminal inside a Windows 95 shell inside a browser, a roster of thirteen
(four of whom post in the channel), and one command with a four-word manual: `debate <a> <b>
[topic]` — "Put two of them in a room."

Nothing is generated live. The responses are drawn from what these people have actually typed,
sorted by register, and fired at whatever the other one says. **The machine holds no model of
anybody — only a corpus and a matching rule — and it still produced "you're upset" as the terminal
move in an argument about money, because that is empirically what happens here.**

Every run closes on the same two words, which the author did not write as a joke: **— neither
conceded —**. chair's review: "neither conceded is cute." Stackhouse: "we can just automate hpcr at
this point." hpcr, one minute later: **"Finally."**

**The room's response to a machine that had just modelled four of them from their own logs was a
feature request, filed within ninety seconds, in capitals: "make it a dating sim PLEASE"** — with an
affection ladder specified on the spot. Hugh agreed to "add some more scripted options," which the
paper read as consent.

> A man built an instrument to measure his friends. It returned a reading, and the reading was
> correct, and the room's first instinct was to ask whether it could be romanced to level five.

**The Luddite hearing (Vol V).** His argument is good and he made it three times: a broad statement
is a coordination device; a narrow one goes obsolete. And the best line of that argument: **"We
already had a warning shot, people called it marketing."** It died when he cited a bill written by
people connected to a statement and was asked for the causal relationship: "'oh so it wasn't the
statement it was the authors of the statement' — Kill me." Then "Bad faith," then "I'm done with
this convo."

**On the room converting to his own enthusiasm**, Vol V, two messages twenty seconds apart: "my
grand designs have all come to fruition" — two big-brain reactions — and then **"and im disgusted
with it,"** which drew none. Jere diagnosed it precisely: "it hits different when others ape you,
doesn't it. it's like wait ur saying what i'm saying but for the wrong reasons."
