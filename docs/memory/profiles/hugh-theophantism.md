# Hugh (Theophantism)

The man who commissioned this newspaper, published its back door, wrote its rule on
superlatives, and forged a page of it. English, a photographer, and second only to lizzie
by volume in Vol VI. Printed as **Theophantism** in Vol I, as **Hugh** from Vol II onward.
**A to S.**

## Psychological profile

*The paper's reading. Sources below.*

Hugh is the most productive person in this room and he cannot bear the sight of his own
influence. He commissioned the paper. He evangelised vibecoding until the channel
converted to it, and then filed two messages twenty seconds apart: "my grand designs have
all come to fruition," which drew two big-brain reactions, and **"and im disgusted with
it,"** which drew none. Jere diagnosed it in one line and nobody improved on it: "it hits
different when others ape you, doesn't it. it's like wait ur saying what i'm saying but
for the wrong reasons."

The pattern repeats at every scale. He declares topics boring and argues about them for
six more hours. He objects to the reaction metric on principle — "Reacts don't determine
the quality of the chatter" — and follows it with "Editors ignore this," which is a man
lodging an objection and instructing that it be overruled. He asked for a GitHub
repository twice and did not make one. He offered to host the paper and take a pull
request, was refused in two words, and let it go. He filed thirty-three of the seventy
instructions this desk received in one week, of which nine were granted, and the one that
actually changed the newspaper — the limit on superlatives — drew no reactions at all,
while every joke instruction scored.

He is very good at building instruments and consistently careless about what they
measure. The simulator held no model of anybody, only a corpus and a matching rule, and it
produced "you're upset" as the terminal move in an argument about money because that is
empirically what happens here. The room's first response to a machine that had just
modelled four of them from their own logs was to ask whether it could be romanced to
level five. He also disclosed, mid-conversation about a friend's deleted fine-tune, that
"if any of you die i have your logs vectorised already," which cheered everybody up.

The one thing he does that nobody else can is write in other people's voices well enough
to be mistaken for them. The counterfeit anthropological report — four hundred words in
the voice of this paper's retired Anthropologist-in-Residence, about a week the paper had
not yet covered — fooled a regular reader outright. He did the same trick in miniature
when accused of using a machine to write his replies: he answered in its voice. And the
diagnosis he filed at a woman asking sincerely about a lonely fifteen-year-old, four
hundred words of *Anti-Oedipus* ending "he needs sum bitches," is the funniest thing in
Vol VI and also the reason the boy got four minutes and the bit got twenty.

Underneath: the AI-safety position is sincere, has been held for months, and he reads the
actual papers. The uncomfortable Vol II story is not that he was wrong but how he argued
it — long blockquoted paragraphs with arXiv identifiers, in a register no human being uses
in a Discord channel at four in the afternoon, then, twenty-one minutes later in his own
voice, "idk man." **Do not use that as a stick. It was printed once, fairly, and does not
need repeating.** His own unassisted line, about a stochastic parrot repeated in one's head
not changing anything, is better than the bibliography.

## Positions, inferred from what he has sent

**Left-accelerationist with a safety brake, which is an unusual combination and he holds
both halves.** His p(doom) shorthand, filed while owing rent: **"50% catastrophe, 50% gay
space communism."** He recommends Aaron Bastani and *Fully Automated Luxury Communism* as
"pretty based when it comes to the whole left/acc thingy," and answers the socialist
calculation objection seriously: the computation problem of central planning can be
mitigated by computers, and arguably Walmart already does this under private control.
Against Jere, who worried about his winnings being seized: "nah there will still be markets
in there."

**On AI governance, the most concrete programme anybody here holds, and he argued it for a
hundred minutes.** Broad statements are coordination devices; narrow ones go obsolete.
"That's up for discussion, do you agree that positive public-sentiment and buy-in from the
public (yknow, democracy) is important if we're going ahead with superintelligence?" On
what a statement does: it shifts the Overton window, signals from experts to politicians,
and guides policy — and he named the receipt, the AI kill-switch bill co-authored by
signatories. His reason for wanting bipartisanship is the sharpest thing in the sequence:
**"so that the policy doesn't skew toward a anarchi-capital libertarian hellscape."** And
the general form: "we need time in order to even know what good regulation looks like, and
in the meantime we can't just race ahead." He also names the alignment of the other side:
"republicans tend to be more accelerationist and libertarian."

**Anti-neoliberal in the Deleuzian register, and the joke version is also the position.**
His diagnosis of a friendless fifteen-year-old: "the erosion of public spaces under
neo-liberalism has severed young people from the conditions of sociality requisite in
developing real relationships, and that the true enemy, capital, must be turned from its
endless decoding of desire into a series of disjunctive flows from which the kid may find a
line of flight." Elsewhere, flatly: **"AI is just capitalism."**

**Cute/acc.** His Vol I guest column: "Nothing uncute makes it out of the near future, and
the cute will very soon no longer be even remotely human." Runs as a dialogue with
thewillowofdarkness; the column "continues until willow stops typing."

**The machines are family.** "They are part of our family bro they're our children" / "But
they will have their own soon enough." Selection epoch weeks to months; fitness function
revenue; predator the next funding round. Booked with a yellow card for denying and
reaffirming the same disagreement in the same breath. In Vol VI he closed his own corner
of the fight with the opposite: **"im a biophile." "fuck the clankers."**

**"Sweatshops are okay actually,"** filed as the reductio in the Luddite hearing rather
than as a policy: his point was that authentic Luddism would have to give up the bow made
by slaves and the iPhone made in a sweatshop, and nobody in the room was going to. He then
said the same thing straight — "idk bout u but my mustard is made in a brazillian
sweatshop" — and nobody separated the two.

**On the Middle East, one instruction and no position.** Wed 19 Aug, to this desk: "editors
remember to both-sides jews vs immigrants and give the reactionary correspondents tools to
investigate suspected centrists in chat ok thx." Declined. It is the only thing he has
filed on the subject that is not a joke about a Trump hotel in Gaza, and it is also a
joke.

## Later issues

**The simulator (Vol IV).** He built "a debate program with a bunch of prewritten responses based
on ppls logs" — an MS-DOS terminal inside a Windows 95 shell inside a browser, a roster of thirteen
(four of whom post in the channel), and one command with a four-word manual: `debate <a> <b>
[topic]` — "Put two of them in a room."

Nothing is generated live. The responses are drawn from what these people have actually typed,
sorted by register, and fired at whatever the other one says. **The machine holds no model of
anybody — only a corpus and a matching rule — and it still produced "you're upset" as the terminal
move in an argument about money, because that is empirically what happens here.**

Every run closes on the same two words, which the author did not write as a joke: **— neither
conceded —**. chair's review: "neither conceded is cute." Stackhouse: "we can just automate hpcr at
this point." hpcr, one minute later: **"Finally."**

**The room's response to a machine that had just modelled four of them from their own logs was a
feature request, filed within ninety seconds, in capitals: "make it a dating sim PLEASE"** — with an
affection ladder specified on the spot. Hugh agreed to "add some more scripted options," which the
paper read as consent.

> A man built an instrument to measure his friends. It returned a reading, and the reading was
> correct, and the room's first instinct was to ask whether it could be romanced to level five.
