# Front-page architectures

The shape of page one, before any of the parts go in. Two have shipped. Both
work. The paper has never varied *within* either of them, which is the gap.

---

## A. Twin leads under a full-width nameplate

**Ran:** Vol I No 1, page 1.

Blackletter nameplate across the full measure with a boxed ear on each side.
Below the folio bar the page splits into two co-leads separated by a vertical
rule — a three-column lead on the left, a two-column second lead on the right —
and a bottom register carrying a boxed graphic feature, the contents box and the
quote box.

The argument for it: two stories get front-page weight, and the bottom register
absorbs the small material that has nowhere else to go. It reads as a newspaper
immediately, before a word is read.

Watch for: the two leads competing. The left lead needs a visibly bigger
headline or the eye does not know where to start.

---

## B. Single dominant lead over a rail

**Ran:** Vol I No 4 and Vol I No 5, page 1 — identically, which is the problem
this folder exists to fix.

Accent rail, stacked nameplate, folio bar, a week-in-numbers band, a wide
contents strip, then one centred headline running the full measure with a deck
and a ledes strip beneath it. Body in three columns with a fourth narrow rail on
the right carrying a reversed stat panel and two tinted boxes.

The argument for it: one story gets the whole front page, and the rail gives the
numbers and the quotes a permanent home instead of interrupting the copy.

Watch for: the rail becoming a filing cabinet. Three boxes down the right-hand
side every week is the template trap. If the rail carries the same three things
in the same order as last issue, use a different architecture.

---

## Not yet tried

Worth building when the material suits. Each of these is an ordinary broadsheet
device, not an experiment:

- **Picture-led.** One photograph or screenshot at four or five columns across
  the top of the page, headline beneath it, story wrapped round. The right answer
  for any week whose defining moment was an image. The paper has never once done
  this — Vol I No 3, No 4 and No 5 each ran a front page with no picture at all.
- **Wide lead over a brief strip.** Lead across the top four columns, then a
  horizontal band of five or six one-paragraph briefs across the foot, each with
  its own kicker. Good for a week of many small things and no large one.
- **Split down the middle.** Two stories of equal weight, equal headline size,
  a heavy rule between them, arguing with each other across the gutter. Good for
  a week the room genuinely divided.
- **Single-story page.** One story, no second lead, no rail, given the whole
  page including a large graphic. Reserved for a week that deserves it; the
  restraint is the statement.
- **All-briefs page.** No lead at all. Twenty items, each two sentences, laid out
  as a grid. Works once, as a joke about a week where nothing happened.
